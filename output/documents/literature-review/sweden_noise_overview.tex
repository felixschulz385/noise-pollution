Noise pollution is commonplace, but its consequences for human capital formation are less studied than those of air pollution or heat. Our goal is to provide first causal, long-run evidence on the impact of noise externalities on human capital formation. For an overview of central papers, see \autoref{tab1}.

\section{Previous evidence}

{\bf{The most relevant papers: Identification of noise impacts.}} There are two main issues in identifying the causal impact of noise: residential sorting and air pollution concurring with noise pollution. A few key papers convincingly address these issues using either wind direction and noise from non-polluting, electric trains \parencite{greenhillNoisePollutionInfant2026}, sound barriers construction around roads \parencite{morettiTrafficNoiseExternality2025}, or noise proofing of houses \parencite{lindgrenSoundEnvironmentHealth2021}. These papers study the impact on health at birth \parencite{greenhillNoisePollutionInfant2026}, hospitalizations because of cardiovascular disease \parencite{lindgrenSoundEnvironmentHealth2021}, and property prices \parencite{morettiTrafficNoiseExternality2025}, but not on human capital formation. Studying impacts on human capital while addressing the two main identification issues is our goal. 

\bigskip

\noindent {\bf{Limited evidence on productivity and cognition.}} Evidence on cognition and productivity highlights the potential of noise also impacting broader human capital formation. In a field experiment, \textcite{deanNoiseCognitiveFunction2024} shows experimentally that a 7dB increase in workplace noise reduces textile-worker output by about 3 percent and reduces an index of attention, memory, inhibition and further measures capturing cognition. The most convincing design studying impacts on cognition among children is that of \textcite{hyggeProspectiveStudyEffects2002}, who exploit the Munich airport relocation as a natural experiment. Testing schoolchildren matched on socioeconomic characteristics before and after the airport move, they find that children newly exposed to aircraft noise show impaired reading and long-term memory, while those who lost exposure show improvements. Other papers find consistent cross-sectional associations of higher noise with worse memory and reading skills \parencite{clarkEffectsRoadTraffic2010,hainesChronicAircraftNoise2001,klatteEffectsAircraftNoise2017,lercherAmbientNoiseCognitive2003}. Many other papers also measure the link between noise and educational outcomes in the cross-section, but cannot address the two main identification issues. 


\bigskip

\noindent {\bf{Noise likely deteriorates sleep, well-being, and health.}} Existing literature establishes a plausible link between noise and health indicators. Apart from distraction by noise, that is, a direct impact on cognition and learning discussed above, this is likely one of the most important mechanisms. 

Two economics papers seem particularly important here: First, \textcite[working paper]{greenhillNoisePollutionInfant2026} constructs a new noise measure by using night-time seismometer data capturing electric train traffic in combination with wind direction. \textcite{greenhillNoisePollutionInfant2026} finds that nighttime rail-noise during pregnancy reduces birthweight and an index of newborn health. Second, \textcite[working paper]{lindgrenSoundEnvironmentHealth2021} finds that improved house insulation against noise reduced hospitalizations for cardiovascular disease.

Beyond these two most closely related papers, \textcite{boesAIRCRAFTNOISEHEALTH2013} exploit two unexpected flight-regulation changes in Zurich and find that noise increases sleeping problems and headaches. The causal estimates are larger than cross-sectional estimates because of sorting \parencite{boesAIRCRAFTNOISEHEALTH2013}.  Further, \textcite{beghelliHealthBenefitsReducing2023} exploit an unannounced reallocation of early-morning landings at Heathrow and find that prescription spending for sleep disorders, anxiety, depression as well as respiratory disease falls when noise drops \parencite[see also][]{wangImpactAeroplaneNoise2022}. And, finally, \textcite{krekelDoesPresenceWind2017, krekelWindTurbinesHave2023} use a differences-in-differences design, finding lower well-being close to wind turbines but no health effects \parencite[see also,][]{zouWindTurbineSyndrome2017}. A broader public health and economics literature points in the same direction but are either laboratory experiments or less well identified, relying more on self-reported exposure and on cross-sectional variation \parencite[see, e.g.,][]{vanpraagUsingHappinessSurveys2005, basnerWHOEnvironmentalNoise2018, basnerNocturnalAircraftNoise2008, griefahnNoiseEmittedRoad2006, bodinAnnoyanceSleepConcentration2015, trimmelEffectsLowIntensity2012}. 

\section{Data and Identification}

\autoref{tab2} provides an overview of potential data sources.

\subsection*{Variation in Noise Exposure: Barriers and Dwelling Insulation}

We identify exposure to noise pollution using the staggered rollout of mitigation technology: Trafikverket, the Swedish transportation authority, provides dwellings and neighborhoods with sound insulation and noise barriers, respectively. The BoViT program covers all provided insulation and barriers, including the exact dwellings treated and the date of provision. Data on dwelling insulation are, however, not publicly available and must be requested from Trafikverket. A smaller, publicly available subset covers only noise barriers, and construction dates are missing for many of these. 

Supplementary, publicly available data on dwelling, road, and rail positions let us determine which dwellings are located close enough to a road or railway to plausibly be eligible for treatment--i.e., to construct a sample of at-risk dwellings used in the staggered rollout design--and we can additionally request modeled background noise exposure (for all of Sweden) and historical traffic density.

\subsection*{Outcomes: Health and Educational Attainment}

We plan to study health and education outcomes based on administrative data. For instance, in adolescence, National Patient Register diagnosis codes would let us study mental health, sleep disorders, and insomnia. At birth, the Medical Birth Register would provide birthweight and other neonatal health measures for children exposed to noise in utero. In adulthood, register data would let us study diagnosed cardiovascular diseases including hypertension \parencite[similar to][]{lindgrenSoundEnvironmentHealth2021}.

Alongside health, students' learning outcomes will be shaped by noise pollution they are exposed to at school and at home. With publicly available school-level data, we can identify the effects of noise barriers next to school buildings. Individual-level test scores from Skolverket, matched via the geocoded population register (RTB), would let us identify the effects of home noise insulation. Finally, access to the labor-market register would let us identify longer-term outcomes.


\subsection*{Potential Identification Strategies}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Noise barriers (similar to \cite{morettiTrafficNoiseExternality2025}):} exploit the staggered construction of road/rail noise barriers, comparing dwellings and schools just inside vs.\ just outside the newly shielded area--separately for home exposure (residential DiD) and school exposure (e.g., school-level DiD using national test data), following \textcite{morettiTrafficNoiseExternality2025}. 
\item \textbf{Sound insulation (similar to \cite{lindgrenSoundEnvironmentHealth2021}):} exploit the staggered timing of BoViT insulation as in \textcite{lindgrenSoundEnvironmentHealth2021}, but compare treated dwellings to not-yet-treated eligible dwellings rather than to never-treated ones.
\end{itemize}

For either strategy, we may use the timing of insulation or barrier construction relative to a child's birth or school-entry date to construct historical exposure measures to isolate in-utero and early-childhood exposure windows. Across these designs, we can account for dwelling/school fixed effects, cohort or year-of-birth fixed effects, and family socioeconomic status from the population register.

A key issue is that property values move with noise levels \parencite{allroggenPlanesOverheadHow2025, morettiTrafficNoiseExternality2025}. But this seems potentially addressable as we would know sorting based on individual-level data and depending on exposure timing (e.g., via sibling fixed effects). Also, barriers built around schools would likely lead to much less sorting and/or wealth effects than closeby where people live.
